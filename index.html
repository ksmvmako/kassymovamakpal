01 / The Problem

Missing-child searches are not only about police response — they are also about public awareness.

A person may be physically close to a missing child and still never know a search is taking place. This project focuses on that information gap: how search information travels, who receives it, and who never sees it at all.

ILLUSTRATION · HOW A SEARCH CAN BE MISSED

One verified alert. Not one guaranteed audience.

A search may begin officially, but residents nearby might still never see a clear, current and location-relevant notice.

Missing children are a serious child-safety issue in Kazakhstan. Official reports by the Ministry of Internal Affairs and the Astana Police Department show that thousands of cases require search and response every year. These numbers alone do not prove system failure — but they do show that this is not a rare or marginal issue.

Importantly, Kazakhstani law-enforcement guidance states that families do not need to wait 24 hours before reporting a child missing. In principle, the police should begin responding immediately. Yet immediate reporting does not automatically guarantee broad public awareness.

The question is not whether Kazakhstan has search organizations — it does. It is whether residents can find one timely, clearly verified public alert and know how to help safely, rather than relying on scattered posts and forwarded screenshots.

Official search notices can circulate through police channels, social media and forwarded messages. A forwarded screenshot may no longer show whether the case is active, the details are verified, or the search concerns someone nearby. This project examines how such information gaps might affect public awareness.

A search network already exists: LIDER.KZ ↗ publishes missing-person notices and accepts search requests. BalaSafe would support existing search teams with verified local alerts and structured tips, not replace volunteers.

READING THE DATA

What the figures reveal — and what they don't.

Tap one question to reveal the evidence gap.

01 Can we compare regions?

Not reliably. The reports count different things — calls, searches or children located — across different months. A larger figure is not automatically greater risk.

02 How quickly are children found?

The reviewed announcements do not provide comparable regional 24-hour recovery figures or median search times. Recovery totals alone cannot measure response speed.

03 Who sees the alert?

Public reports do not establish alert delivery, verified tip quality or reach among rural residents and different language groups. These are important questions for a future pilot, not proven inequalities.

Data limitation: this project uses selected published reports, not an integrated national case database. Protecting children and witnesses also limits what should be made public.

03 / Current State of Research

Can a better alert system actually help find missing children?

Research points to three connected problems: agencies must coordinate, citizens must know when and how to respond, and public alerts must not put vulnerable children at greater risk. The evidence does not yet prove that AI-powered alerts produce faster recoveries.

From Kazakhstan to international research

In Kazakhstan, official statistics and operational information describe search activity, but the academic literature located for this review does not offer a direct evaluation of a nationwide missing-child geolocation alert system. A UNICEF-supported 2021 study of cross-border child-protection information exchange between Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan provides relevant regional context about case management. It is not an impact evaluation of missing-child public alerts.

For evidence on missing-child investigations and public appeals, this review therefore draws on peer-reviewed studies from the United Kingdom, Canada and Europe. Their insights are informative, but their institutional contexts are not identical to Kazakhstan's.

APPROACH 01 · INSTITUTIONAL COORDINATIONInformation moves between people and institutions—not just screens.CLICK TO READ STUDIES & FINDINGS+

Waring et al. (2023) analysed 24 interviews with police, social services, local authorities and care-home staff across three UK boroughs. They identified unclear roles, inconsistent language and difficulties sharing information. Monaghan et al. (2025) surveyed the wider literature on inter-agency responses and found promising practices, including designated contacts, shared information and training, but limited evidence proving which interventions improve outcomes. Waring et al. (2023) also examined dedicated missing-person teams as an organizational alternative to relying on technology alone.

What we know: coordination can be constrained by organizational boundaries. What remains contested: whether new technology, clearer responsibilities or dedicated human teams deliver the greatest improvement. Qualitative interviews and reviews identify mechanisms, but cannot by themselves demonstrate a causal reduction in search times.

Read: Waring et al. (2023), inter-agency working · Monaghan et al. (2025), scoping review · Waring et al. (2023), dedicated teams

APPROACH 02 · PUBLIC ALERTS AND CITIZEN PARTICIPATIONMore views do not automatically mean more useful sightings.CLICK TO READ STUDIES & FINDINGS+

Solymosi, Petcu and Wilkinson (2020) studied 1,008 missing-person appeal tweets from Greater Manchester Police to investigate what influences redistribution on social media. A separate Canadian study, #Missing to #Found (2020), examined 373 missing-person tweets from 15 police services and their public engagement. These studies make alert visibility measurable—but retweets, likes and comments are engagement indicators, not confirmed recoveries.

Hunt, Ioannou and Synnott (2022) explored why members of the public say they would or would not report a possible sighting after seeing missing-child appeals. Their findings shift attention from receiving an alert to making a decision to act. Still, stated intentions based on hypothetical appeals may differ from real behaviour during an emergency.

What we know: the wording and distribution of appeals can shape engagement, while public decisions depend on more than exposure. What is missing: direct evidence that sending a geographically targeted notice to more phones creates more verified leads or shorter searches.

Read: Solymosi et al. (2020) · #Missing to #Found (2020) · Hunt et al. (2022)

APPROACH 03 · TECHNOLOGY, SAFETY AND VULNERABILITYAn alert can help a search—and create new risks.CLICK TO READ STUDIES & FINDINGS+

Howlings and Solymosi (2023) interviewed eight professionals investigating missing children. Their pilot study documents the potential value of social media alongside concerns about immediate and long-term harm, and calls for safer ways to manage posts and remove outdated appeals. They propose investigating text-based machine learning, but do not establish its effectiveness in real searches.

A 2021 peer-reviewed study from the EU-funded ChildRescue research project drew on expert interviews across European countries and examined the information needs of missing-child responses, particularly vulnerable unaccompanied migrant minors. It also notes that robust evidence on the effects of child alert systems remains scarce.

What we know: child safety and data minimization cannot be secondary design features. What remains uncertain: which sensitive details should reach the public, how inaccurate sightings should be verified, and whether automated processing improves outcomes without amplifying harm.

Read: Howlings & Solymosi (2023) · ChildRescue-related study (2021) (publisher page)

THE RESEARCH GAP

What has not been demonstrated for Kazakhstan?

Existing scholarship supports the importance of coordinated institutions, well-designed public appeals and safeguarding. Yet the sources reviewed here do not test whether police-approved, location-based alerts with AI-assisted multilingual message processing increase the number of useful, verified public leads or shorten response time in Kazakhstan.

BalaSafe KZ addresses this gap as a policy and system proposal—not a proven intervention. Its value should be evaluated against an ordinary non-AI alert service using measures such as delivery speed, verified-tip quality, staff workload, language accessibility, privacy and safeguarding outcomes. Faster alerts alone are not sufficient proof of better child protection.

ACCOUNT SAFETY · VOLUNTEER COORDINATION

Not everyone gets the same permissions.

Open public information must be safe and accessible. Posting case details, sending private tips, and joining search teams require progressively stronger verification.

01

Visitors

Read approved public notices without submitting identifying details. No identity check just to stay informed.

02

Registered residents

Confirm an email or phone number before submitting tips or subscribing to district alerts. Spam limits and moderation reduce fake submissions.

03

Verified volunteers

Extra identity checks and organizer approval are needed to join district search groups or access assignments. Only assigned teams see operational instructions.

04

Police and coordinators

Organization-issued credentials, role-based access, audit logs and two-factor authentication control publication and case management.

AI's supporting role: group authorized volunteers by district, availability and language; flag suspicious repeat submissions for review; and summarize incoming reports. A human confirms identity, reviews tips and assigns every search task.

06 / Final Verdict

Should Kazakhstan build BalaSafe KZ?

The evidence supports investigating better public information delivery. It does not yet establish that an AI-powered system is necessary, affordable or safer than a well-designed non-AI alternative.

PROVISIONAL PROJECT VERDICT · CONDITIONAL

Start with verified alerts. Test AI only where it earns its place.

Based on the reviewed evidence, a controlled pilot could be considered only after the current alert ecosystem is audited, a competent institution accepts legal responsibility, and the benefits of adding AI are tested against ordinary location-based notifications. There is not enough evidence to recommend nationwide deployment.

DECISION FIGURE · WHEN TO SAY YES

Adopt only under conditions.

The site’s verdict is not a full endorsement. It argues for a cautious, limited and testable public-safety approach.

Conditions for a limited pilot

01Police and child-protection authorities approve a public-notice protocol and a safeguarding veto for high-risk cases.
02Run one geographically limited pilot using opt-in alerts and no public disclosure of exact child locations.
03Compare a human-only geofencing service with an AI-assisted trilingual version using the same approved cases.
04Evaluate delivery time, verified tips, staff burden, language parity, errors and any child-safety complaints.
05Publish aggregate evaluation results and establish independent child-protection review.

When the answer should be “no”

×No lawful and reliable source of police-approved alerts is available.
×No institution can independently verify public tips or investigate erroneous alerts.
×Children's sensitive details or witnesses' identities cannot be protected or deleted promptly.
×The AI system performs worse than a simpler non-AI platform in accuracy, costs or equitable access.
×The tool encourages vigilantism, stalking or the return of children to unsafe environments.
