01 / The Problem

Missing-child searches are not only about police response — they are also about public awareness.

A person may be physically close to a missing child and still never know a search is taking place. This project focuses on that information gap: how search information travels, who receives it, and who never sees it at all.

Missing children are a serious child-safety issue in Kazakhstan. Official reports by the Ministry of Internal Affairs and the Astana Police Department show that thousands of cases require search and response every year. These numbers alone do not prove system failure — but they do show that this is not a rare or marginal issue.

Importantly, Kazakhstani law-enforcement guidance states that families do not need to wait 24 hours before reporting a child missing. In principle, the police should begin responding immediately. Yet immediate reporting does not automatically guarantee broad public awareness.

The question is not whether Kazakhstan has search organizations — it does. It is whether residents can find one timely, clearly verified public alert and know how to help safely, rather than relying on scattered posts and forwarded screenshots.

Volunteer groups such as LIDER.KZ already distribute search notices, while official public guidance also describes notices shared through social-media groups. However, a notice appearing in a Telegram chat or Instagram post is not necessarily received by someone near the search area. Forwarded posts may also be missing context about whether the information is current and officially approved. Whether this creates measurable gaps in awareness is a question this project investigates, not an established national statistic.

How the information gap works

A search alert may move through several actors before it ever reaches the wider public.

1
Family reports a child missing.
2
Police register and process the case.
3
Volunteer organizations may help spread approved alerts.
4
Citizens only help if the information reaches them in time.

The visibility problem

Even motivated citizens may never see a missing-child announcement unless they already follow volunteer channels or encounter the alert through social media.

The coordination problem

When information is scattered, residents may not know whether a case is active, what details are verified, or how to report a sighting safely.

Research Question

How do existing information-sharing practices between police, volunteer organizations, and citizens shape public awareness and participation in missing-child searches in Kazakhstan, and under what conditions could AI-assisted location-based alerts improve this coordination?

This framing keeps the project grounded in social science. The central issue is not whether AI is impressive, but whether information flows, institutional coordination, and public participation could be improved in a way that is useful, ethical, and realistic.
